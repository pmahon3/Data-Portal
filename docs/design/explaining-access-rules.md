# Show the outcome, then test the examples

reviewed: false. Synthesized 2026-10-31 from three research notes, the earlier user-guide research, and a reading of `docs/user-guide/how-access-works.md`, `docs/writing-the-user-guide.md`, `.vale/` and `backend/tests/test_policy_guide.py` in INN-Data-Portal (development).

The best practice is consistent across vendors and research. First, name the parts of a grant. Second, state in one sentence how grants combine. Third, spell out the edge cases where people actually go wrong: items added later, renames and moves, folders shown but not opened. Fourth, put the outcome in front of the reader in plain words, next to the thing it affects. Usable-security studies trace most permission errors to one gap: the rules people *write* differ from the access that *results*. Interfaces that show the resulting access lifted task accuracy from 25% to 100% and from 6% to 100% ([Reeder thesis, 2008](https://www.csd.cmu.edu/sites/default/files/phd-thesis/CMU-CS-08-143.pdf)). The INN portal's model already has the simplifications that research recommends: positive grants only, a folder treated as one protected space, and few actions ([Smetters & Good, 2009](https://cups.cs.cmu.edu/soups/2009/proceedings/a15-smetters.pdf)). Its "How access works" page already beats every research-data product checked, because it states what a path covers, says files added later are included, and backs its examples table with a test. To be excellent, the page needs four changes. It should open with a short "In short" box that holds the whole decision rule. The additive rule needs its own section with a two-policy example and the consequence "you can't carve out part of a wider policy". Renaming and moving should become a stated rule rather than a bullet among the surprises. And the checked table should grow by a few rows covering the misconceptions the research names, such as `write` without `delete`, a renamed folder, and a hidden sibling folder. Most of this needs no change to the test. Two things the research suggests belong to the product, not the page: an effective-access view beside each folder, and a preview before saving that names the extent of a broad grant. They are listed separately at the end.

## Leading products name the parts, state the combination rule, then troubleshoot

Azure RBAC's overview is the closest template for the portal. It opens with four plain "Allow one user to…" examples. It then says "a role assignment consists of three elements: security principal, role definition, and scope", gives each its own heading, and combines them in one worked assignment. Next comes a section on multiple assignments: "Azure RBAC is an additive model, so your effective permissions are the sum of your role assignments". Its worked example shows a Reader assignment having "no impact" beneath a wider Contributor one. It closes with a numbered procedure framed as help "if you're trying to troubleshoot an access issue" ([Azure RBAC overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/role-based-access-control/overview)). Each piece maps onto the portal. Principal, role and scope become person, action, and instance plus path. The additive section becomes the portal's no-deny rule. Google Drive orders its page as concepts, then tasks, then troubleshooting. It states the additive consequence as a negative: you can't give someone less on a file than they have on its folder, so to restrict, move the file elsewhere ([Google Drive](https://support.google.com/drive/answer/2494822)). SharePoint defines "parent" and "child" inline, then runs three named scenarios on one fictional company ([SharePoint](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sharepoint/what-is-permissions-inheritance)). GitHub, GitLab and Box each lead with a role-by-action check-mark matrix. GitHub's page never says how team, base and direct access combine. GitLab hangs its conditions in about 78 footnotes ([GitHub](https://docs.github.com/en/organizations/managing-user-access-to-your-organizations-repositories/managing-repository-roles/repository-roles-for-an-organization); [GitLab](https://docs.gitlab.com/user/permissions/)).

AWS IAM shows the cost of completeness. It opens with a clean three-bullet rule set ("By default, all requests are implicitly denied…"), then follows it with a long ordered walk through policy types and exceptions that is hard reading for end users ([AWS deny/allow](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/IAM/latest/UserGuide/reference_policies_evaluation-logic_policy-eval-denyallow.html)). The portal needs only the three bullets, because it has no deny and no policy types to order.

Research-data tools set a low bar. Globus explains sharing as eight steps with ten screenshots and never says what a path covers or what happens on rename ([Globus](https://docs.globus.org/guides/tutorials/manage-files/share-files/)). Nextcloud has no permission table at all ([Nextcloud](https://docs.nextcloud.com/server/latest/user_manual/en/files/sharing.html)). **Renaming and moving are the worst-documented area everywhere**. In Drive and Box, access follows the container, and the move effects appear mostly in admin pages or community forums ([Workspace admin](https://knowledge.workspace.google.com/admin/drive/moving-content-from-drive-shared-folders); [Box community](https://community.box.com/admin-forum-25/how-to-move-a-folder-without-disrupting-permissions-34733)). On moves the portal behaves much as Drive does: an item takes the access of where it lands. Renaming is the difference that needs stating. A portal policy is tied to the text of a path, so a renamed folder leaves its policies behind. The notes found no vendor page that documents what renaming does to access, so the page has to state this as a rule, not a footnote.

Three vendors provide a "check access" tool. AWS's policy simulator, GCP's Policy Troubleshooter and Azure's **View my access** each take who, what and which action, and return the decision plus the grant responsible. GCP lists, for each binding, whether it matches the principal, includes the permission, and meets conditions. It can also start from the error a refused user just saw ([GCP troubleshooter](https://docs.cloud.google.com/policy-intelligence/docs/troubleshoot-access); [AWS simulator](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/IAM/latest/UserGuide/access_policies_testing-policies.html); [Azure check access](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/role-based-access-control/check-access)). Each concept page links to its tool rather than embedding it.

## People fail on the combined result and the edges, not on single rules

The best-replicated finding is the stated-versus-effective gap. In Windows XP's NTFS dialog, users could not see effective permissions or group membership. Accuracy fell to 25% on one task, and **goal errors** (not knowing what needed doing) dominated ([Maxion & Reeder, 2005](https://www.academia.edu/7781285/Improving_user_interface_dependability_through_mitigation_of_human_error)). WebDAV users made the same confusion. Only 10% managed a task that needed a group change, even with effective permissions shown ([Cao & Iverson, 2006](https://cups.cs.cmu.edu/soups/2006/proceedings/p20_cao.pdf)). Deny precedence was the worst case: 6% completed one task with the native Windows interface ([Reeder, 2008](https://www.csd.cmu.edu/sites/default/files/phd-thesis/CMU-CS-08-143.pdf)). The portal has no deny, so it avoids that error class entirely. The page can say so in one line and never mention deny again.

The edges are where the portal's model can still go wrong. Ten years of DocuShare logs show 95% of objects simply inherited their folder's settings. About 12.5% of explicitly set objects looked like **inheritance failure**: a change made to a folder that never reached its contents. Users treat a folder and everything in it as "a common protection domain" ([Smetters & Good, 2009](https://cups.cs.cmu.edu/soups/2009/proceedings/a15-smetters.pdf)). The portal's `folder/*`, "including files added later", matches that model. Its exceptions are where it breaks the model, and the page has to name them. Ticking every file in a folder still gives per-file policies, so later files stay hidden. A renamed folder leaves its policies behind. Reeder found file moves to be a source of policy errors. He argues that a moved file should immediately take on its new parent folder's rules, and found NTFS's deferred inheritance less predictable ([Reeder, 2008](https://www.csd.cmu.edu/sites/default/files/phd-thesis/CMU-CS-08-143.pdf)). The portal already does what he recommends: a moved item is covered at once by whatever policies cover its new path. The page should say so, because it is a strength. Errors run in one direction. Facebook settings matched users' intent only 37% of the time, and when wrong they **almost always exposed more than intended** ([Liu et al., 2011](https://www.mpi-sws.org/%7Egummadi/papers/imc081s-liu.pdf)). In 100 people's own Dropbox and Drive accounts, 13% wanted to unshare at least one sampled file ([Khan et al., 2018](https://archive.cps-vo.org/node/62694)). Ordinary users split read from modify and delete on their own ([Mazurek et al., 2010](https://www.pdl.cmu.edu/ftp/Storage/CHI2010.pdf)). Smetters & Good question whether a separate delete control earns its keep. Since the portal keeps `write` and `delete` apart, the page must show that `write` does not include `delete`, or readers will assume it does.

Four kinds of representation help. **Effective access shown in the interface** produced the largest gains measured: Salmon went from 25% to 100%, and the Expandable Grid from 6% to 100% with 70% less time on another task ([Reeder et al., CHI 2008](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1494103)). **Placement matters as much as presence.** Access information shown under the item got 52% of permission subtasks done, against 30% for a sidebar and 24% for nothing ([Vaniea et al., 2012](https://reitermk.github.io/papers/2012/PST.pdf)). **Stating the intended outcome**, and letting the system derive the rules, reached 100% on all four tasks, against 100/70/30/10% for the ACL editor ([Cao & Iverson, 2006](https://cups.cs.cmu.edu/soups/2006/proceedings/p20_cao.pdf)). **Plain language beats terse grids for novices.** The same Grid applied to privacy policies lost to a well-written prose policy. Reeder concludes natural language "may be the most effective means for explaining nuanced policy concepts to users who have never encountered those concepts before" ([Reeder, 2008, §8.5.1](https://www.csd.cmu.edu/sites/default/files/phd-thesis/CMU-CS-08-143.pdf)). For administrators, Bauer et al. found that policy makers "have no way of knowing if it is correct or not" and that emailed access lists went unread for months. They recommend recording the reason for each change and letting people preview and approve changes ([Bauer et al., 2009](https://reitermk.github.io/papers/2009/CHI.pdf)). Two limits apply. **No study tested worked examples in access-control documentation, broad-grant warnings, or "what if" previews in isolation.** The recommendations for those are extrapolations from the effective-access and oversharing results. No study tested the specific words "inherit", "recursive" or "wildcard" either. The wording rules below follow from model-level findings, not from word tests.

## Model first, rules second, a handful of key examples under test

Diátaxis treats explanation (study) and reference (work) as different modes. Reference must be "austere", consulted rather than read, and "wholly authoritative". It may carry examples, but should link to explanation rather than absorb it ([Diátaxis: reference](https://diataxis.fr/reference/); [explanation](https://diataxis.fr/explanation/)). One page can hold both if they sit in separate sections. The house style already classes this page as reference, and at about 1,200 words including markup it does not yet need splitting. The signals to split are a rules section longer than the explanation, or readers linking mainly to rule anchors. The inverted pyramid puts the conclusion first, so a reader can stop at any point and still have the model ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/inverted-pyramid/)). AWS's three-bullet summary is the model for an "In short" box whose rules are never wrong, only incomplete. Each rule then follows one template: a one-sentence statement, an example, and a counter-example. The page's existing "Not `patient-70/`" is exactly the counter-example pattern Diátaxis asks for. Examples need the general rule beside them, because "examples only highlight a few points, you have to infer the generalizations yourself" ([Fowler](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/SpecificationByExample.html)).

For the checked table, every mature approach separates key examples written for readers from exhaustive cases written for machines. doctest warns that "filling your docstrings with obscure test cases makes for bad documentation" ([Python doctest](https://docs.python.org/3/library/doctest.html)). Cucumber says declarative examples "read better as living documentation" ([Cucumber](https://cucumber.io/docs/bdd/better-gherkin/)). FitNesse decision tables put inputs on the left and the outcome on the right, and allow unchecked comment columns ([FitNesse](https://fitnesse.org/FitNesse/UserGuide/WritingAcceptanceTests/SliM/DecisionTable.html)). The portal's table already follows that shape. The style guides add three table rules: no blank cells (write *Not applicable*), brief one-line cells with few columns, and specific headers ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/scannable-content/tables)). Introduce each table with a full sentence and never merge cells ([Google](https://developers.google.com/style/tables)). Aim for one typical row and one counter-example per rule, roughly 8 to 20 rows. The full matrix stays in `test_access_control.py`. A diagram earns its place only for an ordered evaluation with early exits, which the portal does not have ([AWS overview](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/IAM/latest/UserGuide/reference_policies_evaluation-logic.html)). A plain-text tree of what Bob sees is enough. It also avoids two problems with Mermaid: whether it renders on the wiki is unconfirmed ([github/docs#15727](https://github.com/github/docs/issues/15727)), and the repo requires a `%% check:` marker plus a check in `tools/tests/test_docs.py` for every diagram.

## The current page is strong on rules and weak on combination and edges

The page states the path forms with counter-examples, says "including files and folders added later" in bold, explains path-only visibility from the viewer's side, and lists real surprises. All three studies would approve. What it lacks follows from the research. It has no summary of the whole decision. The additive rule is two sentences under a parts table, with no worked combination and no "you can't remove part of a wider policy". The rename rule is the third bullet among the surprises. `write` excluding `delete` appears only implicitly, in the technician example. The page says "the person" while the house style says "you". Two claims carry no test: "Access is by name" (no rename or move test exists in `backend/tests/`) and "a download link handed out before lasts up to 15 minutes". The surprises marker lists tests for neither. These claims either get tests or move to a section marked for review only. The "Examples, read aloud" section has a claim marker but no tests, so it stays review-only wherever it goes. Nothing in the repo links to anchors on this page, so its headings can change freely today. After the restructure they should be kept stable.

The test fixes what the table can look like. The parser takes the text after `<!-- check: policy-examples` up to the first blank line, then drops three lines: the rest of the marker line, the header and the separator. So the header row must sit on the line straight after the marker, the table can have no blank lines inside it, and any lead-in sentence goes above the marker. Cells map to a five-argument parametrize **by position**. Renaming the headers costs nothing. Adding a "Why" column, reordering columns, or splitting into one table per rule needs a test change. Each cell holds only the value, with backticks at the ends at most. For "refused" rows the test calls only `normalize_rule_path`, so their blank cells can say *Not applicable* with no test change. "shown in tree" always evaluates with the `read` action (the third argument to `rule_shows_folder` is hard-coded), so the upload and delete trees can't be shown yet. Each row is one policy, so two policies combining cannot be expressed. The Azure-style additive example has to be prose under review, or the test needs multi-policy rows.

### Wording rules

| Avoid | Say instead | Why |
|---|---|---|
| inherit, recursive, propagate | "this folder and everything in it, including what's added later" | Users hold a folder-as-one-space model; propagation terms are developer vocabulary (Smetters & Good) |
| wildcard, glob, pattern | "a folder path ends in `/*`"; "`*` on its own means everything" | Paths are literal; the regex misconception is already the commonest refusal |
| resource, principal, object, grant, permission, rule | file, folder, person, **policy** (the UI's word, **Policies**) | One term per concept; system terms mislead (Cao & Iverson) |
| deny, denied, blocked by | "no policy gives it" | No deny exists; say so once in section 2 |
| effective permissions | "everything your policies give, added together" | Names the result without the jargon |
| traverse, list permission | "shown in the tree: the name only, nothing inside" | Explain from what the viewer sees |
| matches | covers | Matches the path table's own verb |
| "the person" | "you" for the holder's view (what you can do, what you see). Named people (Alice, Bob) in examples. "An administrator" for who saves policies. | House style says "you"; both audiences reason from the holder's side |

General rules: every rule gets a counter-example in the same sentence or row. Answers are "yes", "no" or "refused", never blank. Lead-in sentences end in a full stop, following Microsoft's choice. Sentences stay under 25 words; the Vale rule only suggests at 30. Avoid "just" and "simply": Vale's `Portal.Filler` fails the build on them, including "just a dot". The "In short" rules must read true even without the detail below.

## Conclusion

The portal's simple model is a documentation asset. With no deny, no policy types and literal paths, the whole decision fits in one sentence, and the page should open with it. Vendors with richer models cannot do that. The real work is at the edges, where users' folder-as-one-space intuition breaks: per-file ticks, renames and hidden siblings. Each edge can be one checked row. That makes the test-backed table more than a correctness guard. It becomes the place where the misconceptions the research names are refuted with the portal's own decision code.

The same sentence form should serve both the page and the product. If "Means" in the read-aloud table becomes the exact wording of a save preview, then the documentation, the preview and the test all describe one outcome in one voice. That is the doc-side equivalent of the effective-access display that did best in the studies. Of the UI changes, the who-has-access view beside the folder has the strongest evidence and is the one to put first to Patrick.
